\begin{abstract}

Early AI programs were often also theories of how people think, and
programs that wrote programs were, in part, theories of how people
program. Here we report on the reanimation of two such programs that
offer two different theories of programming and were developed for
quite different purposes. The first, Herbert Simon's Heuristic
Compiler (1961), was an experimental model of programming as
problem-solving, applying the means-ends analysis methods of the
General Problem Solver to investigate the nature of the programming
task. The second, KBEmacs (1984), was a working prototype of an
intelligent programming assistant developed by MIT's Programmer's
Apprentice project, built on the view that expert programmers
recognize and combine familiar patterns, or ``cliches.''  It let a
programmer build up programs by combining cliches through a stylized
English command language. We approach both through Executable
Archaeology, the study of historical computational systems by running
their original code, a process we call ``reanimation.'' Our reanimation of the Heuristic Compiler
reproduces the compiled code printed in Simon's 1963 paper, and our
reanimation of KBEmacs reproduces the SQRT sequence of its December
1984 self-test. In order to highlight the reflexivity of this project,
we tried to have most of the detailed effort in these reanimations
carried out by a modern AI-assisted programming tool, Anthropic's
Claude Code, although it started from a significant base of existing
software, including a modern IPL-V interpreter, modern Common Lisp,
and an existing Common Lisp port of the Lisp Machine's object system.
The explicit, executable theories of programming that both systems
embodied are largely absent from today's far more capable AI coding
assistants.

\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Executable Archaeology, History of AI,
Automatic Programming, Heuristic Compiler, KBEmacs, Programmer's
Apprentice, Programming Cliches, Psychology of Programming, Cognitive
Models of Programming

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Modern programmers increasingly work alongside AI-based tools built on
large language models and trained on enormous corpora of source
code. Although the scale and fluency of the modern tools are truly
novel, the aspiration of a program that takes part in the work of
programming is nearly as old as programming itself. In the 1950s what
we now call compilation was referred to as ``automatic
programming''~\cite{backus1957fortran,richwaters1988myths}. And within
a few years of the founding of artificial intelligence, researchers
were asking whether the methods of AI could be turned on the task of
programming itself~\cite{balzer1973}.

Programming has also long been studied as a human activity. Weinberg's
\textit{The Psychology of Computer Programming}~\cite{weinberg1971}
treated it as behavior to be observed, and by the early 1980s Soloway
and his colleagues at Yale demonstrated that expert programmers work
from stereotyped ``programming plans'' -- reusable code schemas --
rather than from first principles~\cite{soloway1984}. Most early AI
was intended either as theories of how people do complex things
(e.g.,~\cite{newelletat1958, newell1972hps}), or depended on intuitive
theories of this. And in that framing, a program that writes programs,
or helps a programmer do so, is at least an intuitive theory of
programming, but one that could be helpful to a human programmer.

This paper describes the reanimation of two historical systems that
offer two such theories, though they were built for different ends.
The first is Herbert A.~Simon's \emph{Heuristic Compiler}, written in
IPL-V in 1961 and described in a RAND report that
year~\cite{simonP2349} and in the \textit{Journal of the ACM} in
1963~\cite{simon1963jacm}. Simon treated programming as a `problem' in
the sense in which that term was used in GPS,'' the General Problem
Solver of Newell, Shaw, and Simon~\cite{newell1959gps}: Given a
description of what a routine should do, Simon's program searched for
differences between the routine as it stands and the desired routine,
and applied operators to reduce those differences.

The second system is \emph{KBEmacs}, the Knowledge-Based Editor (in
Emacs). Built at MIT's Artificial Intelligence Laboratory, KBEmacs was
the culminating demonstration of the Programmer's Apprentice
project~\cite{waters1985tr,waters1986aimag,richwaters1987overview}.
The user of KBEmacs guided the computer by indicating standard
``cliches'' to combine in stylized English. KBEmacs supplied the
details and wrote the code by combining together the indicated
cliches. This process was in close accorded with the psychologists
studying programming (e.g.,~\cite{soloway1984}), who proposed that
much of what a skilled programmer knows consists of such familiar
patterns (``programming plans''), each with roles to be filled, and
the skill required to combine them into a correct program.

Although these two systems were separated by a quarter of a century,
by programming language (IPL-V v. ZetaLisp) and platform (large
multi-user computers v. personal Lisp machines), and by research
tradition (the study of human cognition v. applied AI), they stake out
a great deal of the conceptual territory that today's AI coding tools
occupy: How a programming task is specified, where the knowledge and
skill needed to carry it out comes from and where it resides, how work
is divided between human and machine, and how the system checks and
explains what it has done.

We approach the study of these artifacts as exercises in
\emph{Executable Archaeology} employing the core methodology of
``reanimation'': studying historical programs by running them on
emulation stacks from code that is as close as possible to the
original~\cite{lane2025eliza,shrager2026,moewsshrager2026}. The
Heuristic Compiler was transcribed from a 1961 line-printer listing
recovered from Simon's papers at CMU, and run on the Common Lisp IPL-V
interpreter built earlier to run the Logic Theorist. KBEmacs was
transcribed from a four-volume January 1985 printout and run under a
Zetalisp compatibility layer for SBCL, a modern Common Lisp. Both
reanimations were themselves carried out largely by an AI coding
assistant: Anthropic's Claude~\cite{claude}, working in its
command-line form, Claude Code. The division of labor is described in
``Author Contributions and Use of AI''.

\section{Historical Background}
\label{sec:history}

\subsection{The Heuristic Compiler}

The goal of Newell, Shaw, and Simon's work at RAND and Carnegie Tech
in the 1950s was to instantiate theories of human thought as computer
programs. They began with formal logic, and their Logic Theorist (1956)
searched for proofs of theorems in \textit{Principia
  Mathematica}~\cite{LT1957,moewsshrager2026}. Their General Problem
Solver (1959) generalized these methods into a theory of means-ends
analysis as a core cognitive method~\cite{newell1959gps}. Simon's 1963
summary of means-ends analysis is worth quoting in full, because the Heuristic
Compiler implements it almost literally:

\begin{quote}
Given the present and desired objects, find a difference between them.
Next, find an operator relevant to the difference; determine if the
operator can be applied to the present object. If so, apply it. (If
not, describe the objects to which it would apply and transform the
present object into an object of that kind---a new ``desired
object.'') Take the new object thus obtained as the present object and
repeat the process.~\cite[p.~494]{simon1963jacm}
\end{quote}

A proof and a program are not as different as it might seem; a proof
is essentially a program that outputs a theorem from inputs that are
axioms. In this sense (Simon continues), ``a theorem-proving system
can be regarded \ldots\ as a program-writing system. Conversely, if we
can formulate a programming goal as a difference between a present and
a desired object, we can presumably use the same processes \ldots\ to
generate a program''~\cite[p.~495]{simon1963jacm}. The same
observation, in a deductive rather than heuristic setting, later
became the foundation of program synthesis from theorem
proving~\cite{green1969,mannawaldinger1971}.

Simon described the Heuristic Compiler in various publications and
reports in the early 1960s~\cite{simonP2349, simon1963jacm,
  simonRM3588}. His goal was \emph{not} to build an automatic
programming tool: ``no claims will be made for the heuristic
programming procedures described here as practical approaches to the
construction of compilers. Their interest lies in what they teach us
about the nature of the programming
task''~\cite[p.~493]{simon1963jacm}.\footnote{This method is called,
appropriately, ``task analysis'' in \cite{newell1972hps}}

\subsection{The Programmer's Apprentice}

The Programmer's Apprentice (PA) project began at MIT in the
mid-1970s, with Charles Rich and Howard Shrobe's \textit{Initial
  Report on a LISP Programmer's
  Apprentice}~\cite{richshrobe1976}. These researchers were realistic
about the potential capabilities of such an AI-based programming tool
at that time: ``Recognizing that it will be a long time before it is
possible to fully duplicate an expert programmer's abilities, the
project seeks to develop an intelligent assistant system \ldots\ which
will help a programmer in various phases of the programming
task''~\cite[p.~47]{waters1986aimag}.

Over the following decade the project developed three central ideas:
\emph{Cliches} are standard computational methods, such as a linear
search, a report generator, or successive approximation, each with
\emph{roles} that vary from use to use and a fixed surrounding
structure. \emph{Plans} are a language-independent representation of
programs as hierarchical flowcharts with explicit data and control
flow, which Rich formalized in a Plan
Calculus~\cite{rich1981,richwaters1987overview}. And the
\emph{assistant approach} itself was a division of labor wherein the
programmer makes design decisions and the apprentice handles the
routine details.  Waters's dissertation developed the analysis of
programs into plans~\cite{waters1978}. Note how these ideas align with
those of the psychology of programming at that same time, for
example Soloway's programming plans~\cite{soloway1984}.

KBEmacs was the realization of these ideas in a working editor. It ran
on MIT Lisp Machines, and their descendants, inside the Zmacs editor,
and handled both Lisp and Ada. Its best-known demonstration builds an
Ada report generator, \texttt{UNIT-REPAIR-REPORT}, from five commands
such as ``Define a simple-report procedure UNIT-REPAIR-REPORT'' and
``Fill the enumerator with a chain-enumeration of UNITS and
REPAIRS''~\cite[p.~48]{waters1986aimag},\cite{waters1985tse}.

KBEmacs was admittedly a research prototype. Waters wrote that it knew
``only a few dozen cliches'' where a practical tool ``would need to
know many hundreds,'' and that, its ideas having been used ``for
essentially all [they are] worth,'' KBEmacs ``has been
mothballed''~\cite[pp.~54--55]{waters1986aimag}. The project continued
with work on the Plan Calculus, on automated reasoning, and on
requirements and design apprentices, culminating in Rich and Waters's
1990 book~\cite{richwaters1990book}. 

\section{Reanimating the Heuristic Compiler}
\label{sec:hc}

\subsection{The Listing}

The source used here for the Heuristic Compiler is a line-printer
listing headed \texttt{HEURISTIC CODER 7/16/61}, discovered in the
Herbert A.~Simon Papers at the Carnegie Mellon University
Archives~\cite{simonlisting1961}. This listing is dated shortly after
the RAND report P-2349 (\cite{simonP2349}), and is very nearly the
program described in that report. The listing is 94 line-printer
pages, and about 4,470 lines (cards). We call this ``the deck''. The
listing also carries various hand markings indicating a handful of
proposed corrections. The deck contains an executive and two
``compilers''\footnote{Simon uses ``compiler'' at two levels. The
Heuristic Compiler as a whole is a compiler; within it, two
``compilers,'' one for state descriptions and one for functional
descriptions, serve as the operators of its means-ends loop. The final
step, in which the code they build as list structures is printed as
IPL-V text, is done by ordinary print routines, what we would now call
a renderer.}; a natural-language front end (a character scanner,
dictionary, and parser) intended to let the compiler accept English
definitions; and an IPL-V knowledge base.

\subsection{Everything Is a Description List}

The central representational idea of the Heuristic
Compiler is that a
\emph{routine} is an object with attributes stored as an IPL-V
description list (approximately a lisp property list). Each attribute
is one possible definition of the routine: its IPL name, its
\emph{functional description} (i.e., a process name plus arguments,
such as ``INSERT ( ) AT END OF ( )''), its \emph{state description}
(i.e., which cells it affects, and what each holds before and after),
its flow diagram, and its IPL-V code. Within the IPL code rendered in
this representation, IPL-V instructions are themselves description
lists with attributes for each field. And a symbol such as \texttt{J3}
is a description list with a region (the letter J) and a location (the
integer 3). The program never handles IPL code as text. Its compilers
build and transform these structures, and separate print routines
render them as IPL-V text only at the end.

The Heuristic Compiler's executive implements a means-ends
problem-solving loop whose \emph{differences} are kinds of definitions
and whose \emph{operators} are whole compilers (described just
below). The executive finds the closest definition for the routine,
looks up the relevant compiler in a table of connections, applies it,
and tests whether the routine has improved. If the routine has not
improved, the executive gives up.

The \emph{state description compiler} is the most GPS-like part of the
Heuristic Compiler. It compares the input and output states of each
affected cell, classifies the largest difference (addition, deletion,
replacement, or copying), looks up an operator relevant to it, binds
the operator's variables to the actual cells, and then recursively
compiles two subproblems: getting from the original input state to the
required state, and getting from the operator's output state to the
desired output. Its knowledge of IPL-V consists of exactly two
operators, LOAD and REPLACE, each itself a tiny routine with its own
state description.\footnote{The state description compiler in
\cite{simonRM3588} differs from the one we reanimated in representing
states by bare symbols, whereas ours uses described states.}

The \emph{functional description compiler} works by analogy. Given a
routine described as, say, ``INSERT (1) AT END OF THE VALUE OF THE
ATTRIBUTE (0) OF (2),'' it searches the routines already compiled for
one with the same \emph{process}, here ``INSERT (0) AT END OF (1),''
whose code is the single primitive \texttt{J65}. It then adapts that
routine to the new arguments, moving the inputs into working cells,
compiling code to fetch each argument, recursing when an argument is
itself a described computation (a ``determiner''), and wrapping the
result in instructions that save and restore the working cells.

\subsection{Running The Heuristic Compiler}

The deck runs on the Common Lisp IPL-V interpreter originally written
to run the Logic Theorist~\cite{shrager2026,shragerrepo}. Claude
extended the interpreter with several primitives (J-functions) needed
by the Heuristic Compiler but not required by the Logic Theorist, and
added a start card, borrowed from \cite{simonRM3588}. With these
changes, the program runs and reproduces Simon's published examples.

\textbf{Compiling from a functional description.} Given the functional
description of INSERT AT END OF VALUE LIST, the program produces
\texttt{J13 J52 11W2 11W0 J10 11W1 J65 J32 0}, character for character
the code in the 1963 paper~\cite[pp.~499--500]{simon1963jacm}
(Figure~\ref{fig:insert}). A print apparently left in routine U113
shows the code growing one argument at a time, which reproduces the
sequence of stages through which the paper walks its readers. In
Simon's runs, as in ours, the functional description is supplied as a
hand-coded list structure, not as typed English; the phrase is printed
from it, but the compiler matches processes by symbol identity. The
English front end in the deck has not yet been run (see ``Next
Steps'').

\textbf{Compiling from a state description.} Simon's running example
of the state description compiler is J3, SET SIGNAL MINUS: a routine
that replaces the top symbol of the signal cell H5 with the symbol J3
(``minus''). The state description says that H5 goes from \emph{some
symbol on top of the rest of its pushdown list} to \emph{J3 on top of
the same rest}. The compiler finds a replacement difference in a named
cell; finds REPLACE relevant, with its variable bound to H5, giving
\texttt{20H5}; sets up the prefix subproblem ``get J3 into the
communication cell,'' an addition, for which LOAD is relevant, giving
\texttt{10J3}; finds no suffix subproblem; and assembles \texttt{10J3
  20H5 0}, the code in the paper~\cite[p.~498]{simon1963jacm}.

We noted one exception in the reproduced results: Our recovered deck
did not compile J3. The reason for this omission is that our listing's
J3 had both a functional and a state description, but the executive
always prefers the functional description on the assumption that ``it
is easier to compile from a functional description than from a state
description''~\cite[p.~502]{simon1963jacm}. As no already-compiled
routine shares J3's process, the functional description compiler
fails, the executive sees no progress and gives up without trying the
state description. The published paper's J3 result is only produced
when the deck is repaired by removing J3's functional description,
after which the state description compiler produces exactly the
published code. In the 1963 listing (in \cite{simonRM3588}) the
executive is identical but the cards that give J3 a functional
description are numbered as though they were later additions,
suggesting that the paper's J3 came from an earlier version in which,
like our repair, J3 had only a state description.

\textbf{Flow diagrams.} The paper's last section handles routines with
branches and loops by decomposing them into \emph{segments} joined by a
flow diagram. The program reassembles routine J77 (``test whether there
is a symbol equal to 0ACCUMULATOR on list 1ACCUMULATOR'') from its
segments, and decomposes it again into the paper's flow diagram.

\section{Reanimating KBEmacs}
\label{sec:kbe}

\subsection{The Listings}

The surviving KBEmacs source is a January 1985 printout, four bound
volumes of Zetalisp listings, preserved by Kent~Pitman, a member of
the KBEmacs team, whose scans are at
\url{https://github.com/netsettler/netsettlement/tree/main/kbe}~\cite{pitmanscans}. These
cover the system as of KBE version 358.0x (December 1984), although
individual files carry their own dates and versions. Volume~1 opens
with something unusual and extremely useful to an archaeologist: the
results of KBEmacs's automatic self-test, run ``Friday the fourteenth
of December, 1984; 2:25:23 pm'' on the Lisp Machine \emph{Merlin}. The
self-test runs six command sequences, compares the code KBEmacs
generates with expected code, and reports that three sequences (HASH,
SQRT, and SUMLIST) succeeded and three (BUCKET, DELETE, and MEMQ)
failed with ``Bad code generated.''

The 87 files were transcribed (mostly by ChatGPT~\cite{chatgpt}) into
about 34,500 lines of source, available at
\url{https://github.com/jeffshrager/kbemacs}~\cite{kbemacsrepo}.

\subsection{Text and Plans}

A KBEmacs user works in a stylized English command language phrased in
terms of cliches and their roles. The cliche library is written as
code with holes, as in the definition of successive approximation
in Figure~\ref{fig:cliche}.

\begin{figure}[H]
\begin{lstlisting}
(DEFINE-CLICHE SUCCESSIVE-APPROXIMATION

    DESCRIBED-ROLES (INITIAL-APPROXIMATION CRITERION IMPROVEMENT-STEP)
    COMMENT "iteratively improves an approximation until {the criterion} is
             satisfied" ...)
  (LET* ((APPROXIMATION {the input initial-approximation}) )
    (LOOP DO
      (IF ({the criterion} APPROXIMATION) (RETURN APPROXIMATION) )
      (SETQ APPROXIMATION ({the improvement-step} APPROXIMATION) ))))
\end{lstlisting}
\caption{A cliche from KBEmacs's library (\texttt{kbelib.lisp}, 1984).
The braces are KBEmacs read syntax marking \emph{roles}, the parts that
vary from use to use; the surrounding code is fixed.}
\label{fig:cliche}
\end{figure}
Cliches are not stored as text. When the library loads, each
definition is run through KBEmacs's \emph{analyzer}, which turns code
into a plan: a set of facts in a small database describing segments,
ports, data flow, control flow, and roles. In a plan, a \texttt{LOOP}
with a \texttt{SETQ} disappears; what remains is a temporal
composition of a sequence of states. The knowledge-based editor
operates on plans, and after every command the \emph{coder} turns the
plan back into program text, deciding where to nest expressions and
where to introduce variables, and preferring names the programmer has
already used. Waters called this two-way translation the
\emph{representation shift}, and argued that it gave plans locality,
additivity, and modularity that text lacks~\cite{waters1986aimag}.
Because the user may also simply edit the text, KBEmacs must keep text
and plan consistent in both directions.

\subsection{Running KBEmacs (headless)}

KBEmacs depends heavily on the Lisp Machine environment of its time,
including Flavors (the Lisp Machine's object system), the Zmacs editor,
Lisp Machine processes, Waters's own LetS loop notation, and Zetalisp
itself, which is similar to, but not exactly the same as, modern Common
Lisp. Emulators survive for the MIT CADR Lisp Machine (usim) and for
Symbolics Open Genera, but no usable environment compatible with this
KBEmacs release (Symbolics Release 4.5) was available to us. Both
include Flavors, but Open Genera, the closer of the two, is not freely
available, and it corresponds to a Genera release about a decade later
than the one KBEmacs ran on. The reanimation targeted the
\emph{headless} core (the database, analyzer, cliche library,
command language, knowledge-based editor, and coder), leaving out the
editor, windows, actors, and the Ada front end~\cite{kbemacsrepo}.

The 1985 source, with correction of minor typos and transcription
errors, is loaded into SBCL (\url{https://www.sbcl.org}), a modern
Common Lisp, through a Zetalisp compatibility layer that Claude
assembled and debugged. The largest single piece of that layer is
g000001's Common Lisp port of Spice Lisp
Flavors~\cite{g000001}.\footnote{Coincidentally, Spice Lisp, which was
built at CMU, where Simon and Newell taught, begat CMUCL, and SBCL is
a fork of CMUCL.}  The rest of the layer supplies the parts of
Zetalisp and the Lisp Machine that Common Lisp lacks or does
differently. A small test harness was also created to replay the
self-test sequences from the 1984 code and compare the results with
what was expected.

The SQRT sequence (Figure~\ref{fig:sqrt}) builds a square-root routine
from three cliches in seven commands, and then builds a variant with
an extra parameter in seven more. After the first command that names a
cliche, KBEmacs holds the cliche's structure with its roles still
open as holes. Each further command fills a role with code or with
another cliche, whose own unfilled roles appear as new holes. After
seven commands the plan has 32 segments and the program is complete.

\begin{figure}[H]
\begin{lstlisting}

>Implement the improvement-step as an average of [APPROXIMATION]
   and [(// NUM APPROXIMATION)] .
>Implement the criterion as an equality-within-epsilon of
   [(* APPROXIMATION APPROXIMATION)] and [NUM] .
>Implement the epsilon as [0.0001] .

(DEFUN MYSQRT (NUM)
  (LET* ((APPROXIMATION 1))
    (LOOP DO
      (IF (< (ABS (- (* APPROXIMATION APPROXIMATION) NUM)) 0.0001)
          (RETURN APPROXIMATION))
      (SETQ APPROXIMATION (// (+ APPROXIMATION (// NUM APPROXIMATION)) 2.0)))))

The criterion is an equality-within-epsilon ...
\end{lstlisting}

All fourteen commands are parsed correctly and the generated programs are
\texttt{equal} (in the Lisp sense) to the 1984 generated code. These
programs also work: \texttt{(mysqrt 2.0)} returns 1.4142157.

\section{Then and Now: Two Historical Systems and Today's AI Coding Assistants}
\label{sec:thenandnow}

Neither the Heuristic Compiler nor KBEmacs is an ancestor of today's
AI coding assistants in any direct technical sense. These are loaded
with proto-programs or cliches, whereas large language models (LLMs)
are trained on huge volumes of example code, as well as advice
garnered from online forums, such as Stack Overflow. Also, the
Heuristic Compiler and KBEmacs operate on explicit internal
representations of code, whereas LLMs operate on program text rather
than on description lists or plans.\footnote{Certainly an LLM harbours
  a tacit internal representation relevant to the structure of the code
  it is working on, but these are, as far as anyone can tell, unlike
  the explicit representations involved in the reasoning carried out
  by the historical programs studied here.}  Nonetheless the questions
these two systems were built to explore are recognizably the questions
that shape AI-assisted programming today. The comparisons that follow
are conceptual continuities, not architectural equivalences.

\textbf{How is a programming task specified?} Simon's two compilers
differ precisely in how the task is described. A state description
says what the machine looks like before and after, which is close to
what we would now call a specification by examples or by
pre- and post-conditions, and close to the role unit tests play in
directing a modern assistant. A functional description is an imperative
phrase, which is close to a natural-language prompt. Simon drew a
conclusion that remains apt: programmers use both, but ``at the higher
levels \ldots\ a language of functions appears generally the more
natural and powerful,'' and ``the mappings from functional descriptions
to process language are simpler and more direct than the mappings from
state descriptions''~\cite[p.~506]{simon1963jacm}. KBEmacs chose the
functional side and gave it a vocabulary of named algorithms. Today's
assistants accept both kinds of description, and much of the craft of
using them lies in combining a functional request with a test-like
check.

\textbf{Where does the knowledge come from?} Both historical systems
are knowledge-starved by modern standards. The Heuristic Compiler's
state description compiler knows exactly two operators; its functional
description compiler can compile a routine only if a routine with the
same process has already been compiled. KBEmacs knew ``a few dozen''
cliches; Waters estimated that a practical tool would need ``many
hundreds''~\cite[p.~55]{waters1986aimag}. Both systems identified
reuse of known solutions as the heart of the matter: Simon's
functional compiler works by finding an analogous routine and adapting
it, and the whole Programmer's Apprentice rests on the claim that
programs are built from background knowledge. What neither could solve
was acquiring that knowledge at scale. Large language models can be
loosely described as one answer to precisely this bottleneck: in
effect, an implicit cliche library absorbed from an enormous body of
code. This is a metaphor, not a claim about what such models actually
represent, and it comes at the price of having no explicit, inspectable
representation of what a cliche is or what its roles are. 

\textbf{Who does what?} Simon's Heuristic Compiler was not designed as
a practical programming assistant. It was a computational experiment
intended to illuminate the processes involved in programming. KBEmacs,
by contrast, was deliberately conceived as an assistant, leaving
design decisions to the programmer while taking over routine details.
Waters's argument for the apprentice was practical: full automation
was not near, and a semiexpert assistant could be useful at
once. Modern tools have traversed the same axis in a few years, from
completing a line, to writing a function on request, to agents that
plan, edit, and test across a code base with varying degrees of
supervision~\cite{yang2024sweagent,jimenez2024swebench}. The question
of where the programmer's decisions end and the machine's begin,
central to the Programmer's Apprentice, is again a live design
question.

\textbf{How does the system know it is making progress?} The Heuristic
Compiler's executive is a loop: apply a compiler, test whether the
routine has moved to a better kind of definition, and stop if it has
not. Its state description compiler implements difference reduction in
the strict GPS sense. Modern coding agents commonly run a loop of a
similar shape~\cite{yang2024sweagent}: make a change, run the tests or
the compiler, examine the difference between what happened and what
was wanted, and try again. The difference detector is now an executed
test suite rather than a hand-coded comparison of states, and the
operator is a learned model rather than a table of connections, but
the architecture of means-ends iteration is recognizable. The
resemblance goes beyond the loop. A coding agent of this kind is not a
purely neural system: the language model chooses what to do next and
interprets the results, but the processes of compiling, executing,
comparing outputs, tracking changes, and so on, are done by
conventional symbolic programs. In the work reported here these were a
Common Lisp compiler, an IPL-V interpreter, and the 1984 self-test's
own comparison of generated with expected code. Although the
heuristics have moved from a hand-written table of connections into a
learned model, the operators and the difference tests are still
ordinary programs. KBEmacs, for its part, came with a self-test in
which the expected output was code, which is exactly what made it
possible to check its reanimation.

\textbf{Representation and explanation.} The two historical systems
never treated code merely as text. The Heuristic Compiler builds IPL
instructions out of description lists and prints them only at the end;
KBEmacs reasons about plans and regenerates code after every command,
taking pains to keep code and plan consistent when the user edits the
code directly. That explicit representation is what allowed KBEmacs to
describe a program in terms of its design (``The program MYSQRT is a
successive-approximation \ldots''). Today's assistants operate on text
and also explain code fluently, but their explanations are generated
from text, not read off an explicit representation of the design. The
trade-off between the robustness of working with text, which KBEmacs
found painfully expensive to analyze---Waters reports that ``well
over half of all the effort expended on KBEmacs went into implementing
an analyzer and coder''~\cite[p.~55]{waters1986aimag}---and the
inspectability of working with plans, has not gone away.

\section{Technical Next Steps}
\label{sec:next}

For the Heuristic Compiler, the largest piece of unexplored territory
is the English front end: the scanner, dictionary, and parser, and the
routine that converts a parse into a functional description. Simon
credited H.~S.~Kelly with ``linguistic components for the compiler
which are omitted from this report''~\cite[p.~506]{simon1963jacm};
these routines may be them. Running them requires input sentences,
which are not in the listing. 

For KBEmacs, the compatibility layer was debugged against the one
self-test sequence whose expected output was known, so a skeptic may
reasonably ask whether it is tuned to SQRT. The next step is therefore
to run the other five sequences of the 1984 self-test without changing
the layer after seeing their results, aiming to reproduce both the
three successes and the three failures, and to build a new KBEmacs
program that is not among the self-tests. After that comes the Ada
front end, which would allow Waters's
\texttt{UNIT-REPAIR-REPORT} demonstration to be reproduced.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conc}

These two reanimations illuminate a history of AI and programming that
is not simply a progression from primitive to increasingly
sophisticated coding assistants. Simon's Heuristic Compiler
investigated the nature of programming as problem solving. KBEmacs
applied AI to assist human programmers by combining explicitly
represented programming knowledge. Although today's AI coding agents
take on more and more of the work of programming themselves, these
systems are not stages in a single technical lineage. Rather, they
mark three different relationships between machines and programming:
using methods now central to AI\footnote{Newell and Simon themselves
long preferred the term ``complex information processing'' to
``artificial intelligence.''} to understand programming, using AI to
assist human programmers (while also, as we discuss below, proposing a
theory of how they program), and using AI to do more and more of the
programming autonomously. By recovering and executing the original
systems, the methodology of Executable Archaeology allows us to
examine these relationships through the detailed study of the
computational mechanisms themselves.

Like most early AI, both of the historical systems we have reanimated
were, at least in part, attempts to understand how people
program. Simon's Heuristic Compiler treated programming as means-ends
problem solving. The Programmer's Apprentice understood programming as
combining together familiar cliches. And note that Simon's Heuristic
Compiler also depended upon having a knowledge based of what amount to
cliches. More importantly to the present effort, each was a theory of
programming that could be run and tested, and now can again.

Today's AI coding assistants program far better than either the
Heuristic Compiler or KBEmacs, yet the LLMs at their core embody no
comparable explicit theory of cliches or of the problem solving
involved in programming. To some extent, an explicit theory of
programming has reappeared in the harnesses that make these tools work
so well. Their cycle of reasoning, acting on the code, running tests,
and repairing what fails~\cite{yao2023react,yang2024sweagent,schluntz2024agents,claudecodeworks} is a
form of means-end analysis, with the test suite detecting the
differences between what the program does and what it should do. What
the harness leaves to the model, we venture, includes an implicit
theory of problem solving, an implicit library of programming
cliches, and an implicit skill in combining them, all distributed
across the billions of weights that make up the LLM. That this
knowledge is not stated in a form that can be examined is probably
core to what makes these tools so flexible, but it also means that it
cannot be reasoned about. An explicit theory can be. The Logic
Theorist proved theorems by manipulating explicit logical expressions;
the Heuristic Compiler carried the same kind of reasoning over to
explicit descriptions of programs; and KBEmacs's Plan Calculus gave
its cliches a formal semantics~\cite{rich1981}. In each case the
theory of programming was available both to the program, which
reasoned with it, and to its builders, who could analyze it, test it,
and in principle prove things about it. A theory distributed across
the weights of an LLM can only be probed from the outside, by
observing what the model does. One cannot prove anything about what
one cannot represent.

machine time (not counting ChatGPT's extraction of the KBEmacs
listings from the scans).
